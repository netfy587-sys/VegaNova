Chapter 1

Why the ocean matters

Seen from space, Earth looks blue because water covers about seventy-one percent of its surface. Almost all of that water, roughly ninety-seven percent, is in the ocean.

The ocean acts like the planet’s thermostat. It absorbs heat and carbon dioxide, moves warmth around the globe through currents and shapes weather everywhere. Tiny drifting plants called phytoplankton make a large share of the oxygen we breathe, often estimated at about half.

This VegaNova guide begins with the basics so that each chapter builds on the last, from the sunlit surface to the darkest trenches.

We know more about the surface of the Moon than about the floor of our own ocean.

That famous saying is not far from the truth: most of the seafloor has still not been mapped in fine detail, which is why ocean science keeps producing surprises.

Chapter 3

Life in the sea

From microscopic plankton to the biggest animal that has ever lived, the ocean holds an astonishing variety of life.

Whales

The blue whale can grow longer than a basketball court and eats tiny krill by the tonne.

Sea turtles

Seven species travel thousands of kilometres between feeding areas and the beaches where they hatched.

Sharks

Older than trees, sharks keep ocean food webs balanced by feeding on the sick and weak.

Octopuses

With three hearts and remarkable problem-solving, they are among the most intelligent invertebrates.

Reef fish

Colourful fish use coral as shelter, nursery and pantry, forming tightly linked communities.

Deep-sea creatures

In the dark, many animals make their own light to hunt, hide or signal to others.

Chapter 4

Coral reefs: cities of the sea

Coral reefs cover less than one percent of the seafloor, yet they shelter around a quarter of all marine species. Reefs are built by tiny animals called polyps that grow hard skeletons and live in partnership with algae.

Reefs also protect coastlines by softening waves, and they support fishing and tourism for millions of people. They are sensitive to warm water, which can cause bleaching when corals lose their algae partners.

Chapter 6

Explore New Possibilities In The Deep

Reaching the deep ocean is difficult because pressure rises by about one atmosphere every ten metres. At the bottom of the Mariana Trench, it is more than a thousand times what we feel at the surface.

Sonar mapping

Ships send sound pulses to the seafloor and time the echoes, building detailed maps of mountains and canyons.

Remotely operated vehicles

Tethered robots with lights and cameras can work for hours at depths no diver could survive.

Crewed submersibles

Small, strong vessels carry scientists to the abyss to observe life in its own setting.

Autonomous floats

Thousands of drifting sensors measure temperature and saltiness, giving a steady picture of ocean change.

Chapter 7

Look What’s New In Ocean Science

Hydrothermal vents

Discovered in 1977, these seafloor chimneys support life that runs on chemicals instead of sunlight.

New species

Expeditions regularly describe animals never recorded before, especially in the deep sea and around seamounts.

Whale songs

Underwater microphones are helping researchers follow whale migrations and understand their calls.

Plastic-eating microbes

Scientists have found microbes that can break down some plastics, though cleaning the ocean still needs less waste first.

Chapter 8

Challenges and what helps

The ocean faces warming water, acidification, overfishing and plastic pollution. Millions of tonnes of plastic reach the sea every year, harming animals that mistake it for food.

The solutions VegaNova highlights are practical: protected marine areas, well-managed fisheries, cleaner shipping and wiser everyday habits such as cutting single-use plastic and choosing sustainably caught seafood.

Questions people ask

Why is seawater salty?

Rain slowly washes minerals from rocks into rivers, which carry them to the sea. When water evaporates, the salts stay behind.

How deep is the deepest point?

The Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench is about 10,900 metres deep, deeper than Mount Everest is tall.

Do we really breathe oxygen from the ocean?

Yes. Phytoplankton and other marine plants produce a large share of the oxygen in the air, often estimated at about half.

What is bioluminescence?

It is light made by living things through a chemical reaction. In the deep ocean, many animals use it to lure prey, confuse enemies or find mates.
